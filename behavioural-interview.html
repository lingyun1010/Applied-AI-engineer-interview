Behavioural Interview

Six main questions, follow-up questions and the polished answer scripts from our practice sessions.

The polished answers below are reproduced verbatim from our previous discussion. The RAG fix has been evaluation-verified locally; this does not imply that the commit has been pushed or deployed. A small number of follow-ups without a separate polished script contain brief cross-references.
01 · Stakeholder Management

Turning an ambiguous business request into a technical solution

CHARLIE · INTERVIEW QUESTION

Can you tell me about a time when you worked with a non-technical stakeholder who wasn't entirely sure what they needed, and how you turned that into a practical technical solution?

Polished answer · verbatim

One example is a recent project I worked on with Love At Home, a small home care provider in Sydney.

Initially, they approached me with a fairly broad request: they wanted a better website to build trust with potential clients. But after discussing how they actually operated, I realised the problem wasn't just the website. They were relying heavily on referrals, phone calls and emails, and didn't have a structured way to capture enquiries online.

So rather than jumping straight into development, I proposed a simple digital workflow: a professional website, a structured enquiry form, automatic email notifications, and a central place to manage incoming leads.

I started with a prototype so they could review the design and give feedback before implementation. For the backend workflow, I chose Google Sheets rather than introducing a full CRM because it was familiar to their team, low-cost and sufficient for their current needs. I integrated it using a Google Cloud service account with restricted access.

The result was a working website with a new enquiry channel and an automated process for recording and notifying the team about leads. It also reduced the need for manual data entry.

What I learned from that experience is that, especially when working with non-technical stakeholders, the most important step is understanding the underlying business problem before deciding what technology to build.

Follow-up questions

FOLLOW-UP 1That's interesting. You mentioned using Google Sheets rather than a more sophisticated CRM or database. How did you make that decision, and how would you know when the business had outgrown that solution?
Discussed answer

I chose Google Sheets mainly because it matched the client's existing workflow and budget.

Before making the decision, I asked how they were currently managing enquiries. They explained that staff were already manually entering client information into Google Sheets, so the team was familiar with it.

Rather than introducing a new CRM that would require additional cost and training, I decided to automate their existing process.

I also considered security. We used a Google Cloud service account with restricted permissions, so the website integration only had the access it needed.

At their current scale, with around 20 to 40 clients, this was a practical solution.

However, I wouldn't use client numbers alone to decide when to move to a CRM. I'd look at whether the team was spending too much time managing follow-ups, whether they needed more complex workflows, or whether Google Sheets could still meet their access control and data management requirements.

If those needs became more complex, particularly around client records or NDIS-related information, I'd reassess the architecture and consider moving to a more structured system.

For me, it's about choosing technology that's appropriate for the business today, while understanding when it needs to evolve.

02 · Conflict Resolution

Resolving a design disagreement with evidence

CHARLIE · INTERVIEW QUESTION

Tell me about a time when you disagreed with a colleague or stakeholder about a technical decision. How did you handle the disagreement, and what was the outcome?

Polished answer · verbatim

One example was when I was working at EMBL-EBI, where I was responsible for developing an interactive SVG module called the Organ Anatomogram.

I worked closely with an illustrator, who created the SVG graphics, and a bioinformatician, who provided the scientific expertise.

We had a disagreement about the user interaction design. The SVG had a hierarchical structure, and users needed a way to navigate from a parent organ to its child tissues.

I initially proposed a hover-based interaction, while the bioinformatician preferred having a visible rectangular indicator around the interactive area.

We discussed the pros and cons of both approaches over several meetings, but we couldn't reach a consensus.

So I suggested that instead of continuing to debate it, we could build two lightweight prototypes and gather feedback from colleagues across different teams.

Based on that feedback, we eventually agreed to use the visible indicator because we felt it would make the interaction clearer for users.

Although it wasn't my original proposal, I was happy to support the decision because it was based on user feedback rather than personal preferences.

What I took away from that experience is that technical disagreements don't always need to be resolved through persuasion. Sometimes the best approach is to test the options and let the evidence guide the decision.

Follow-up questions

FOLLOW-UP 1That's an interesting approach. Could you be a bit more specific about what the disagreement actually was? What did each person want, and what was your own position?
Discussed answer

This is covered explicitly in the main answer above: hover-based navigation versus a visible rectangular indicator around the interactive area.

03 · Failure & Learning

Diagnosing and fixing RAG location false refusals

CHARLIE · INTERVIEW QUESTION

Can you tell me about a time when something you built didn't work as expected? How did you identify the problem, what did you do to fix it, and what did you learn from the experience?

Polished answer · verbatim

One example comes from EvidMatch, an AI-powered professional matching platform I've been building.

I was evaluating our RAG system, which allows users to ask questions about a candidate's professional profile based on their published information.

During testing, I found that some location questions were being incorrectly refused, even though the location information existed in the profile.

I investigated the retrieval pipeline, including the indexing, chunking strategy and similarity threshold.

I discovered that the information was correctly indexed, but because it was embedded within a larger profile chunk, some short location questions received similarity scores below our threshold of 0.30.

Initially, I considered lowering the threshold, but our evaluation showed that this could allow more irrelevant queries to reach the language model, increasing cost and potentially affecting answer quality.

So instead, I implemented a more targeted solution.

For location-specific questions, the system now detects the intent using lightweight rules and retrieves the location directly from the authorised structured profile, rather than relying on vector similarity.

For all other questions, the existing retrieval pipeline remains unchanged.

After implementing the fix, I ran our answer-level evaluation using real embeddings and the production configuration.

The false refusal rate for location questions dropped to zero across both test profiles, while the hallucination rate remained at zero and all unanswerable test cases were still correctly refused.

The biggest lesson for me was that improving an AI system isn't always about changing the model or tuning global parameters. Sometimes the most reliable solution is to combine deterministic retrieval with semantic search, and validate the impact with systematic evaluation.

Follow-up questions

FOLLOW-UP 1You mentioned that you identified the issue and changed your retrieval approach. How did you validate that your fix actually improved the system, rather than just solving those specific test cases?
Discussed answer

After fixing the location retrieval issue, I ran regression tests to make sure the change didn't negatively affect other question categories.

The location false refusal rate dropped to zero, while the hallucination rate remained at zero and the system continued to refuse all unanswerable questions in our evaluation dataset.

This gave me confidence that the targeted fix improved the intended behaviour without introducing regressions in the measured scenarios.

FOLLOW-UP 2That's a good result. But you've only evaluated the change against two profiles. How confident are you that this approach would scale to thousands of profiles with different structures, languages and incomplete information?
Discussed answer

That's a fair question.

At this stage, I'm confident in the approach for the scenarios we've tested, but I wouldn't claim it's fully validated at scale yet.

We've tested two real profiles, along with some mock profiles, and the results are promising. But I recognise that this doesn't cover the full variety of real-world data.

The advantage of this approach is that we're retrieving location directly from structured profile data, rather than relying on semantic similarity. So the retrieval itself is relatively lightweight and doesn't become more complicated as the number of profiles grows.

However, the bigger challenge is reliability across different data and question patterns.

For example, I'd want to test incomplete profiles, ambiguous location questions, and cases where someone asks about a previous workplace rather than their current location.

I'd also include security regression tests to make sure we're only exposing information the user is authorised to access.

Currently, our platform supports English profiles, so multilingual evaluation would become important if we expanded that support.

Finally, I'd automate these tests as part of our CI pipeline and monitor false refusals, hallucinations and latency as more real profiles become available.

So I'd say the initial results validate the direction, but broader regression testing and production monitoring would be needed before claiming it's reliable at scale.

FOLLOW-UP 3How exactly did structured information improve retrieval?
Discussed answer

After identifying that location questions were being falsely refused, I considered two approaches.

One was to lower the similarity threshold globally, but our evaluation showed that this increased the number of irrelevant queries reaching the language model.

So I proposed a more targeted approach.

For simple factual questions, such as where a candidate is based, we can detect the intent using lightweight rules and retrieve the location directly from the structured profile.

We then pass that information to the language model as trusted evidence, with a verifiable citation.

For other questions, we keep the existing vector retrieval pipeline and similarity threshold unchanged.

The main advantage is that we're not relying on semantic similarity to retrieve a fact that already exists in a structured field.

I'd validate this by running our answer-level evaluation again, checking that location false refusals drop to zero without increasing hallucinations or introducing regressions in other question categories.

04 · Prioritisation & Product Judgement

Choosing an end-to-end MVP over portfolio customisation

CHARLIE · INTERVIEW QUESTION

Tell me about a time when you had multiple competing priorities and limited resources. How did you decide what to focus on, and how did you manage stakeholder expectations?

Polished answer · verbatim

One example comes from EvidMatch, a two-sided platform I've been building for talent and businesses.

At the beginning, I focused mainly on the talent side, especially building professional portfolios. I wanted to make the portfolio more comprehensive and provide different design options.

But as I was building the product, I realised that my development time was limited, and I had to decide whether to keep improving the portfolio or focus on the broader user journey.

So I started talking to potential users at local meetups to understand what they actually needed.

From those conversations, I realised that although a professional portfolio was useful, the more immediate problem for many job seekers was finding relevant opportunities and understanding whether their skills matched the requirements.

Based on that feedback, I changed my priorities.

Instead of spending more time on portfolio customisation, I focused on building an end-to-end MVP, where users could create a profile, discover real job opportunities and use AI matching to assess their suitability.

This gave me a more complete product journey that I could test with real users, rather than continuing to polish one individual feature.

The biggest lesson for me was that prioritisation isn't just about deciding which features to build first. It's about understanding which problems matter most to users and focusing limited resources on delivering real value.

Follow-up questions

FOLLOW-UP 1That's interesting. How did you know that the feedback from just a few people at meetups was representative of your target users? And how did you decide which feedback to act on and which to ignore?
Discussed answer

I wouldn't assume that feedback from just a few people was representative of the entire market.

So rather than relying only on conversations at local meetups, I also researched discussions on LinkedIn and Reddit to understand the common challenges faced by job seekers in Australia.

I was looking for recurring pain points across different sources, rather than acting on individual feature requests.

For example, I found that many job seekers were struggling to find relevant opportunities and demonstrate their skills to employers.

So when deciding what to prioritise, I focused on features that addressed those core problems and supported the end-to-end user journey.

That's why I decided to focus on job discovery and AI matching instead of adding more portfolio design options.

However, I'd still consider this an early hypothesis rather than fully validated market demand. The next step would be to test the actual user behaviour, such as whether people complete their profiles, use the matching feature and return to the platform.

For me, customer feedback helps identify problems, but actual user behaviour helps validate whether the solution works.

FOLLOW-UP 2That's interesting. Can you give me a specific example of an insight you discovered from your funnel analytics, and how that insight changed a product decision?
Discussed answer

One example was when I analysed the user registration funnel on EvidMatch.

I noticed that although people were signing up, many of them weren't completing or publishing their profiles.

At that time, I'd built a highly customised portfolio design with quite a personal style.

So I started questioning whether the design was actually suitable for job seekers who wanted to present themselves professionally to employers.

I also received feedback from users about the publishing experience, including the need for a clearer preview and better information about what would become public.

Based on those insights, I decided to simplify the portfolio design and make it more consistent and professional.

However, I recognised that the funnel data only showed me where users were dropping off, not necessarily why.

So I treated the design issue as a hypothesis rather than a confirmed root cause.

The next step is to compare profile completion and publishing rates after the redesign to understand whether it actually improves user behaviour.

What I learned is that product analytics and qualitative feedback need to work together. The analytics helps identify where the problem is, and conversations with users help explain why it might be happening.

FOLLOW-UP 3So you've redesigned the portfolio based on that hypothesis. How would you measure whether the redesign was actually successful?
Discussed answer

I'd measure success mainly through the profile publication rate.

I'd look at how many users sign up, complete their profiles and actually publish them, and compare the conversion rates before and after the redesign.

However, because EvidMatch is still at an early stage, we have a relatively small user base, so I wouldn't rely too heavily on those numbers yet.

Instead, I also conducted follow-up interviews with users I'd met at local meetups.

I asked them what they found useful, what was confusing, and what prevented them from publishing or sharing their portfolios.

I think combining those two approaches is important. The funnel data helps me see whether user behaviour is improving, while the interviews help me understand why.

As the user base grows, I'd like to run more structured experiments to validate whether the design changes are actually improving conversion.

FOLLOW-UP 4How did you measure user behaviour alongside the qualitative research?
Discussed answer

I also built a user behaviour analytics dashboard to track the conversion funnel, from user registration to profile completion and AI matching usage.

This allows me to see where users drop off and which features they actually engage with.

Rather than relying only on what users say they want, I can use their behaviour to help validate my product decisions.

At this stage, the user base is still relatively small, so I treat the data as an early signal rather than conclusive evidence.

05 · Leadership & Influence

Introducing GitHub Projects through phased adoption

CHARLIE · INTERVIEW QUESTION

Can you tell me about a time when you had to influence a team or drive a change without having direct authority over the people involved? How did you get their buy-in?

Polished answer · verbatim

One example was when I was working at EMBL-EBI.

Our team was using a separate project management tool to manage the backlog and plan our development work, while our code was hosted on GitHub.

I noticed that GitHub Projects could integrate more closely with our development workflow, so I thought it might be worth exploring.

I introduced the tool during one of our weekly meetings and demonstrated its features to the team. I also discussed the idea with our line manager and got his support to try it.

However, we weren't sure whether GitHub Projects would fully meet our needs, so I suggested a gradual transition rather than migrating everything immediately.

We kept our existing backlog in the original tool. Then, during our weekly sprint planning meetings, we started moving selected features into GitHub Projects as we planned the upcoming work.

This allowed the team to try the new workflow without disrupting our existing planning process.

Over time, we found GitHub Projects convenient to use, particularly because it was more closely integrated with our development work.

Eventually, the team decided to stop using the previous tool and adopt GitHub Projects.

What I learned from this experience is that driving change doesn't always require authority. It's about demonstrating the benefits, getting the right stakeholders involved, and making the transition manageable so people feel comfortable adopting something new.

Follow-up questions

FOLLOW-UP 1Was everyone immediately supportive of moving to GitHub Projects, or did you face any resistance? How did you deal with people who were comfortable with the existing tool?
Discussed answer

The phased transition and line manager buy-in are addressed in the main answer above. We kept the old backlog and introduced selected features during weekly sprint planning instead of migrating everything upfront.

FOLLOW-UP 2How did you know the new tool was actually better? What criteria did you use to evaluate whether the team should fully adopt GitHub Projects?
Discussed answer

We didn't have formal quantitative metrics to evaluate the change.

Instead, we focused on how well the new tool fitted into our existing development workflow.

One of the main advantages was that GitHub Projects was more closely integrated with our repositories and issues, which made it easier to manage development tasks in one place.

During the trial period, we gradually started using GitHub Projects in our weekly sprint planning meetings, while keeping the existing backlog in the original system.

This gave everyone an opportunity to try the tool in their day-to-day work and provide feedback.

After using it for a while, we discussed the experience as a team and agreed that GitHub Projects worked well for our needs.

So it wasn't a decision based on formal metrics, but on practical experience, workflow integration and team consensus.

06 · Delivery & Scope Management

Delivering a four-week client pilot and managing scope

CHARLIE · INTERVIEW QUESTION

Tell me about a time when you were responsible for delivering something important under a tight deadline. How did you manage the pressure, prioritise the work, and ensure the quality of the outcome?

Polished answer · verbatim

One example was a four-week pilot project I delivered for Love At Home, a home care provider in Sydney.

The main goal was to build a professional website, introduce an online enquiry form, and help the business start creating educational content using AI tools.

It was quite a tight timeline, especially because the stakeholders were non-technical, and I was also new to the home care industry.

So at the beginning, I created a proposal with clear weekly milestones, deliverables, and responsibilities, including what information I needed from the client.

We had a meeting every Tuesday morning to review progress, clarify requirements and discuss any blockers.

After each meeting, I documented the key decisions and action items, so we had a shared understanding of what needed to be delivered that week.

One challenge was that we needed quite a lot of discussion around the website content, particularly because home care involves sensitive client information.

I made sure we reviewed the content carefully with the client rather than making assumptions about their services or what information could be published.

In terms of prioritisation, I decided to focus on the website and lead form first, because these were the core business requirements.

The video content was less urgent, and the client wasn't fully ready with their social media strategy, so I scheduled that work later in the project.

At the end of each week, I presented the deliverables, gathered feedback and addressed any necessary changes before moving on.

By the end of the pilot, we'd delivered the website and enquiry workflow, and the client subsequently decided to continue working with me on an ongoing basis.

What I learned from this experience is that managing a tight deadline isn't necessarily about working faster. It's about defining priorities early, managing dependencies, and keeping stakeholders aligned throughout the delivery process.

Follow-up questions

FOLLOW-UP 1You mentioned you had a fixed four-week deadline. What would you have done if, in the final week, the client requested a significant new feature that couldn't realistically be delivered within the agreed timeline?
Discussed answer

Actually, I encountered a similar situation during the Love At Home project.

We had agreed on a four-week delivery plan with a clearly defined scope in the proposal and contract.

During the project, the client mentioned that they were spending a lot of time on repetitive administrative tasks, particularly preparing invoices manually, and they were interested in automating that process.

I recognised that it was a genuine business problem, but building a new automation workflow would require additional discovery, development and testing.

So rather than adding it to the existing project and potentially affecting our agreed deliverables, I suggested that we treat it as a separate project.

I also proposed an ongoing support arrangement for smaller IT-related tasks, while more substantial automation work could be scoped and quoted separately.

The client was happy with that approach, and we agreed to continue our collaboration.

For me, scope management isn't about simply saying no to new requests. It's about understanding their business value, being transparent about the trade-offs, and finding a realistic way to deliver them without compromising the original commitments.
